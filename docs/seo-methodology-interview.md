# SEO Methodology Interview

## Purpose

This document is not the SEO standards. Its purpose is to capture the reasoning behind SEO decisions. The answers collected here will be used to build and refine `docs/seo-standards.md`.

Do not worry about formatting or completeness. Answer each question based on experience, even if the answer changes over time.

---

## Section 1: Overall SEO Philosophy

### 1. What does SEO mean to you?

SEO to me means showing what the user is looking for by making sure your content matches user intent and answers common questions that the user has. The goal for me is to create ensure that I get the right users to my website and once they are there I start to build trust and can convert them by calling or submitting a form fill.

### 2. What separates a great local SEO website from an average one?

The content that is on the website is the biggest thing. Having great content vs just okay content can make the biggest difference between the quality of users that get to your website.

### 3. What are the biggest SEO mistakes you see local businesses make?

The biggest thing is not dominating their backyard meaning where they are located based. Many business try to primarily target the larger metro area like Phoenix when they are physically located in Mesa and that performance doesn't work as well especially since Phoenix is highly competitive.

### 4. If you had to rank the most important SEO factors for a local service business, what would they be?

For me, SEO starts with website architecture. If the structure of a website isn't logical, everything else becomes more difficult. I focus on building a website where every page has a clear purpose, supports a specific search intent, and fits into a well-organized hierarchy. From there, I prioritize creating genuinely helpful content that answers homeowners' questions instead of simply targeting keywords. Strong internal linking is equally important because it reinforces topical authority, helps users discover related information, and distributes authority throughout the site. I also believe the website and Google Business Profile should work together, with consistent local signals, service areas, and business information. Technical SEO, including clean URLs, structured data, XML sitemaps, canonical URLs, and fast performance, should be built into the framework rather than treated as an afterthought. Finally, I view SEO as an ongoing process of continuous improvement. Every project should strengthen not only the current website but also the framework used to build future websites.

### 5. What principles do you refuse to compromise on?

I believe every SEO decision should put the user first. Every page should exist for a reason, provide genuine value, and satisfy a unique search intent. I avoid creating duplicate pages that compete with one another simply to increase page count because I believe quality and purpose are more important than quantity. I also believe SEO starts with a strong, scalable website architecture that makes sense for both users and search engines. Whenever possible, I prefer data-driven, reusable systems over one-off solutions so improvements benefit every future website instead of solving only a single problem. I won't compromise on honest, natural writing or fabricate credibility through fake statistics, awards, or exaggerated claims. Finally, I believe important SEO decisions should be documented and turned into standards so the framework continues to improve over time and future developers or AI assistants can build websites consistently without relying on memory or repeated prompts.

## Section 2: Website Architecture

### 1. Why do you structure websites the way you do?

I structure my websites in a way that creates content clusters to help with internal linking. For service page examples I create the plumbing service page and then put subpages under that so that they all link to each other and back up to the plumbing service page. That changes depending on the vertical that is being targeted.

### 2. What is the purpose of the homepage?

The homepage serves as the foundation of the website and represents the primary office location and primary market we serve. Its purpose is to clearly communicate who we are, what services or verticals we offer, where we provide those services, and why homeowners should choose our company over the competition. The homepage should immediately answer the visitor's most important questions while establishing trust, credibility, and a strong first impression.

The homepage should introduce every major service offered by the business and provide clear pathways to the individual service pages, allowing visitors to quickly find the information they need. It should also reinforce the areas we serve, highlight the company's unique value, showcase customer reviews and trust signals, answer common questions, and make it easy for users to contact us. Every section should have a purpose, whether it is educating the homeowner, building confidence, improving navigation, or encouraging conversions.

From an SEO perspective, the homepage should establish the overall topical authority of the business while targeting the primary office city and supporting the rest of the website through strong internal linking. It should serve as the central hub that connects services, service-area pages, and supporting content into a logical site architecture. Like every page on the website, the homepage should be written for people first and search engines second, ensuring the content is helpful, trustworthy, and focused on creating the best possible user experience.

### 3. What is the purpose of a service page?

A service page exists to become the authoritative page for a specific service and rank for the search intent associated with that service. Its primary goal is to help homeowners understand the service, answer the questions they are likely to have, and build confidence that our company is the right choice to solve their problem. The page should educate first, reduce uncertainty, and clearly explain what the service is, when it is needed, what the customer can expect, and why our approach is different.

Every service page should be written for the user first and search engines second. SEO should naturally support the content through clear page structure, helpful information, internal linking, and relevant keywords, but the focus should always remain on creating the best possible resource for someone searching for that service. The page should also establish trust by demonstrating experience, setting clear expectations, and addressing common concerns without making exaggerated or unsupported claims.

Beyond ranking and educating, a service page should be designed to convert visitors into customers. Strong calls to action, easy ways to contact the business, trust signals, frequently asked questions, related services, and logical next steps should all work together to help the homeowner feel comfortable taking action. Every section on the page should have a purpose, whether it is improving understanding, building trust, strengthening SEO, or increasing conversions. If a section does not add value to the user or support one of those goals, it should not be included.

### 4. What is the purpose of a service-area page?

A service-area page is built to serve as the local landing page for a market we actively serve but where we may not have a physical office. It should be similar to the homepage in structure and purpose while being tailored to the specific city or service area. The page should clearly communicate who we are, what services or verticals we offer in that market, why homeowners in that area should choose us, and how to contact us. It should provide enough unique, locally relevant information to satisfy the search intent of someone looking for our services in that city without simply duplicating the homepage.

A service-area page should strengthen our local SEO strategy by targeting surrounding markets while supporting the authority of the primary office location. These pages should complement, not compete with, the homepage and core service pages. They should reinforce our internal linking strategy, connect visitors to related services, and help search engines understand the geographic areas we serve. Every service-area page should have a clear purpose, provide genuine value to the user, and represent a market where we can realistically deliver our services.

### 5. When should a page exist?

A page should only exist if it serves a clear purpose for both the homeowner and the business. Every page should target a unique search intent, provide valuable information, and support the overall website architecture. Creating pages simply to increase page count or target every possible keyword is not a sustainable SEO strategy. If a page cannot offer something unique or improve the user's experience, it should not be created.

Before creating a new page, I ask several questions. Does this page answer a question that is not already covered elsewhere on the website? Will it help a homeowner make a more informed decision? Does it support a service we actually provide or a market we genuinely serve? Will it strengthen the website's internal linking and topical authority without competing with an existing page? If the answer to these questions is yes, then the page likely deserves to exist.

Every page should have a clearly defined role within the website. It should either establish authority for a service, represent an important service area, support the customer journey, answer common questions, or strengthen the overall SEO strategy. If a page exists, it should contribute meaningful value to both users and search engines while fitting naturally into the website's architecture. Every page should earn its place on the website rather than exist simply because a keyword has search volume.

### 6. When should a page NOT exist?

A page should not exist if it does not provide unique value to the user or if it creates duplicate search intent. I don't believe in creating pages simply because a keyword has search volume or because it increases the total number of pages on a website. Every page should have a clearly defined purpose within the site's architecture. If that purpose already exists on another page, the new page is unnecessary and will likely weaken the website rather than strengthen it.

I also avoid creating pages for services we do not offer, markets we do not realistically serve, or topics that cannot be supported with meaningful, helpful content. Thin pages, duplicate pages, and pages created solely for SEO rarely improve the user experience and often create unnecessary maintenance over time. If a page cannot provide information that is different from existing content or answer a unique question for the homeowner, it should not be built.

Before deciding to create a new page, I evaluate whether improving an existing page would better satisfy the user's needs. In many cases, expanding or strengthening an existing page is more valuable than creating another page targeting similar keywords. My goal is to build a website where every page has a unique role, supports the overall SEO strategy, and contributes to a better experience for both users and search engines. If a page cannot meet those standards, it should not exist.

### 7. How do you avoid duplicate search intent?

I avoid duplicate search intent by ensuring every page has a clearly defined purpose and targets a unique audience or search query. Before creating a page, I evaluate whether it provides information or value that doesn't already exist elsewhere on the website. If two pages are trying to rank for the same service in the same market or answer the same question, they are likely competing with one another rather than strengthening the website. Instead of creating additional pages, I look for opportunities to improve the existing page or restructure the website so each page has a distinct role.

A good example of this philosophy is the relationship between primary office pages and service-area pages. The homepage and core service pages are designed to represent the primary office location and should be the authoritative pages for those services in that market. Creating additional location pages for the same city would result in duplicate search intent and force search engines to determine which page is most relevant. Instead, service-area pages are reserved for surrounding markets where they serve a unique purpose and target a different geographic audience.

I also use website architecture and internal linking to prevent duplicate intent. Every page should fit into a logical hierarchy where it supports, rather than competes with, other pages on the site. If a new page cannot clearly answer the question, "What unique purpose does this page serve that no other page already fulfills?" then it should not be created. My goal is for every page to strengthen the website's overall topical authority instead of dividing relevance across multiple pages targeting the same search intent.

### 8. Why should the primary office city be represented differently from surrounding service areas?

The primary office city should be represented differently because it serves a different purpose within the website's architecture than the surrounding service areas. The homepage and core service pages should establish the business's authority in the market where it is physically located and operates. These pages become the canonical destination for users searching for those services in the primary city. By consolidating authority into the homepage and service pages, the website creates a stronger signal for both users and search engines instead of spreading relevance across multiple pages targeting the same market.

Surrounding service areas serve a different purpose. Their role is to demonstrate that the business provides services in nearby communities while addressing the unique search intent of homeowners in those locations. Service-area pages allow the website to expand its geographic reach without creating duplicate pages that compete with the homepage or core service pages. Each service-area page should represent a distinct market with locally relevant information while supporting the authority of the primary office location through thoughtful internal linking.

This approach creates a cleaner website architecture, avoids duplicate search intent, and makes it easier for search engines to understand which pages should rank for the primary market versus surrounding service areas. It also provides a better user experience by giving every page a unique purpose. Rather than creating multiple pages competing for the same service in the same city, the website uses its homepage and core service pages to own the primary market while allowing service-area pages to expand visibility into nearby markets. This philosophy creates a more scalable framework that can be applied consistently across future local service business websites.

## Section 3: URL Strategy

### 1. How do you decide URL structure?

I decide URL structure based on the physical locations a business operates from and the markets it actively serves. The website architecture should reflect the real-world structure of the business rather than simply creating pages for every possible keyword or city. The primary office location becomes the foundation of the website, with the homepage and core service pages representing that market. Surrounding service areas are then organized beneath the location architecture to expand geographic visibility without creating duplicate search intent.

Every URL should have a clear purpose and fit naturally into the website hierarchy. Service pages represent the primary office market, while service-area pages target surrounding cities that the business actively serves. This creates a logical structure that is easy for both users and search engines to understand. I avoid creating unnecessary URL variations or duplicate location pages that target the same service in the same market, as they often compete with one another rather than strengthen the website.

My goal is to create a URL structure that is scalable, maintainable, and easy to expand as the business grows. Whether a company has one office or multiple locations, the URL architecture should accurately represent how the business operates while supporting strong internal linking, clear navigation, and a consistent local SEO strategy. Every URL should exist because it serves a purpose for the user, not simply because there is search volume for a keyword.

### 2. When should a city appear in the URL?

A city should appear in the URL only when the page is intended to target a specific geographic market that is different from the primary office location. The purpose of including a city in the URL is to represent a unique service area and satisfy the search intent of homeowners looking for services in that community. If the city does not change the search intent or provide unique value, it should not be included in the URL.

For businesses with a single physical location, the homepage and core service pages should represent the primary office city without requiring the city name in every URL. Surrounding service areas should include the city in the URL because those pages exist to target different geographic markets. This creates a clear distinction between the business's primary market and the additional communities it serves while avoiding duplicate search intent.

For businesses with multiple physical locations, each office should have its own location architecture that accurately represents the markets served by that office. The URL structure should always reflect the real-world operation of the business rather than being driven solely by keyword opportunities.

The decision to include a city in a URL should always support the overall website architecture, improve the user experience, and help search engines understand the relationship between the business's primary office and its surrounding service areas. A city should never be added to a URL simply because it has search volume. It should represent a distinct market with a unique purpose within the website.

### 3. When should a service appear in the URL?

A service should appear in the URL when it represents a distinct service that customers actively search for and deserves its own authoritative page. The purpose of a service page is to become the primary resource for that service, allowing the website to provide detailed information, answer common questions, build trust, and rank for the specific search intent associated with that service.

Not every variation or task should receive its own page. A service should only have its own URL if it offers enough unique value to justify dedicated content and supports the overall website architecture. If the topic can be adequately covered within an existing service page without creating duplicate search intent, then it should remain part of that broader page rather than becoming a standalone URL.

Service URLs should remain simple, descriptive, and easy for both users and search engines to understand. Every service page should fit naturally within the website hierarchy and support related services through internal linking. The decision to create a new service URL should be based on user intent, business value, and the ability to create genuinely helpful content rather than simply targeting additional keywords.

Before creating a new service page, I ask whether it serves a unique purpose, addresses a distinct customer need, and strengthens the website's topical authority. If the answer is yes, it likely deserves its own URL. If not, it is usually better to expand and improve an existing service page rather than create another page that competes for similar search intent.

### 4. How do you decide if something deserves its own page?

I decide whether something deserves its own page by evaluating its purpose, search intent, business value, and how it fits within the overall website architecture. Creating a new page should never be an automatic response to finding a keyword with search volume. Instead, I ask whether the page will provide unique value to the homeowner, answer questions that are not already addressed elsewhere on the website, and support the business's services and markets in a meaningful way.

Before creating a page, I ask several questions. Does it represent a service we actually provide or a market we actively serve? Is there a distinct search intent that isn't already being satisfied by an existing page? Can I create enough unique, high-quality content to make the page genuinely useful? Will the page strengthen the website's topical authority and internal linking without competing with another page? If the answer to those questions is yes, then the page likely deserves to exist.

I also consider the long-term value of the page. Every page adds to the complexity of a website and requires ongoing maintenance, so it should contribute something meaningful to both the user experience and the overall SEO strategy. Whenever possible, I prefer improving and expanding an existing page rather than creating a new one that overlaps in purpose. My goal is to build websites where every page has a clear role, supports the customer journey, and strengthens the overall architecture rather than simply increasing the number of indexed pages.

### 5. What URL patterns do you avoid?

I avoid URL patterns that create duplicate search intent, unnecessary complexity, or do not accurately represent how the business operates. Every URL should have a clear purpose within the website architecture and contribute to the user experience. If a URL exists only because it targets a keyword, but doesn't provide unique value or serve a distinct purpose, I don't believe it should be created.

I avoid creating multiple URL variations that target the same service in the same market, such as having both a core service page and a duplicate location-specific version for the primary office city. I also avoid inconsistent URL structures that make the website difficult to understand or maintain. The URL hierarchy should be predictable, scalable, and reflect the relationship between services, locations, and supporting content.

I also avoid generating pages for cities the business does not actively serve, services the business does not offer, or combinations of services and locations that cannot be supported with meaningful content. URLs should never be created simply because a keyword has search volume. They should represent real services, real markets, and genuine search intent.

As the website grows, I prioritize keeping the URL structure simple and consistent. A well-organized URL hierarchy makes the website easier for users to navigate, easier for search engines to crawl, and easier for developers to maintain. Every URL should reinforce the overall architecture of the website rather than introduce unnecessary duplication or complexity.

## Section 4: Internal Linking

### 1. Why do you internally link pages?

I internally link pages because it can be one of the most important on page optimizations to help users navigate the website and showcase which pages we value most on the website.

### 2. How should authority flow through the website?

Authority should flow to the most important webpages that have the most value in terms of what we actually. Blogs should support service pages and service pages should support each other.

### 3. Which pages should receive the most internal links?

Internal linking should prioritize the pages with the highest SEO value, search demand, and revenue potential. Every internal link should help reinforce the website's most important content rather than being distributed evenly across all pages.

**Priority 1 – Core Service Pages (Highest Priority)**

These pages should receive the greatest number of internal links because they target the highest-volume, evergreen search terms and represent the primary services offered by the business.

Examples:

```
/hvac/
/plumbing/
/electrical/

/hvac/ac-repair/
/plumbing/drain-cleaning/
/electrical/panel-upgrades/
```

These pages should be linked from:

- Homepage
- Service hubs
- Location pages
- City vertical pages
- Related service pages
- Blog articles
- FAQs
- Navigation

**Priority 2 – City Vertical Pages**

These pages target high-value local search intent and should receive strong internal support.

Examples:

```
/locations/chandler/hvac/
/locations/chandler/plumbing/
/locations/chandler/electrical/
```

They should be linked from:

- Location pages
- Core service pages
- Nearby city pages
- Relevant blog content
- Areas Served sections

**Priority 3 – Location Pages**

Location pages act as local hubs and should connect users to the appropriate city-specific service categories.

Examples:

```
/locations/chandler/
/locations/gilbert/
```

These pages should be linked from:

- Homepage
- Locations index
- Nearby location pages
- Footer
- Contact page (where appropriate)

**Priority 4 – Supporting Content**

Blog articles, FAQs, financing pages, promotions, and other supporting content should primarily exist to strengthen the pages above by linking users to relevant service and location pages.

**Internal Linking Philosophy**

Not every page should receive the same number of internal links. Pages with the greatest business value and ranking potential should receive the strongest internal linking signals. Supporting content should help funnel authority toward core service pages, city vertical pages, and location pages, creating a clear hierarchy that benefits both users and search engines.

### 4. When should related services be linked?

Related services should be linked whenever they naturally support the user's journey or provide a logical next step based on the content they are viewing. Internal links should always be relevant and helpful, guiding users to services they may also need rather than being added solely for SEO purposes.

For example, an AC Repair page may link to AC Installation, AC Maintenance, or Ductless Mini Splits, while a Drain Cleaning page may link to Sewer Line Repair, Hydro Jetting, or Leak Detection. This strengthens the topical relationship between services, helps users discover additional solutions, and reinforces the website's overall content structure for search engines.

### 5. When should nearby cities be linked?

Nearby cities should be linked when they provide a logical next option for users and accurately reflect the company's service area. These links help users find services in neighboring communities while strengthening the relationship between geographic markets for search engines. Location pages should prioritize links to nearby cities that are geographically connected rather than linking to every city the company serves.

For example, a Gilbert location page may link to Chandler, Mesa, Queen Creek, and Tempe, but it should not link to every service area across the state. Keeping these links geographically relevant creates a more natural user experience, strengthens local topical authority, and prevents excessive site-wide linking that can dilute internal link value.

### 6. What should never be linked?

Certain pages should not be linked simply to increase internal link counts. Internal links should always have a clear purpose and provide value to the user. Avoid linking to unrelated services, irrelevant cities, duplicate or low-value pages, and any content that does not naturally fit within the context of the page. Overusing internal links or forcing them into content can create a poor user experience and weaken the site's topical structure.

Additionally, avoid site-wide links to pages with little SEO or business value unless they are needed for navigation or compliance. Every internal link should help users find relevant information while reinforcing the website's hierarchy and the relationship between closely related services and locations.

## Section 5: Content

### 1. What makes content genuinely helpful?

Genuinely helpful content is created to answer the user's questions, solve their problem, and help them make an informed decision—not simply to rank for keywords. Every page should provide clear, accurate, and relevant information that reflects the user's search intent, explains the service or topic in an easy-to-understand way, builds trust, and guides them toward the next step.

Helpful content should also demonstrate real expertise and be unique to the business, service, or location it represents. Rather than creating pages to fill space or target every possible keyword variation, the focus should be on delivering content that users would find valuable even if search engines didn't exist. This approach aligns with Google's Helpful Content guidance and creates stronger long-term SEO performance.

### 2. What makes content "thin"?

Thin content is content that provides little or no unique value to the user. This often includes pages with very little information, repetitive content reused across multiple pages, or pages created solely to target keywords without answering the user's questions or helping them make a decision. Simply changing the city name or service name while leaving the rest of the content unchanged is a common example of thin content.

Every page should have a clear purpose and justify its existence by offering information that is useful, relevant, and distinct from other pages on the website. If a page cannot provide meaningful value beyond what already exists elsewhere on the site, it is generally better to strengthen an existing page than create another low-value one.

### 3. What should every service page contain?

Every service page should clearly explain the service being offered, the problems it solves, signs that the service may be needed, the available repair or installation options, and what customers can expect during the service process. The page should build trust by demonstrating the company's experience, highlighting its guarantees or differentiators, and answering common questions that help users make an informed decision.

From an SEO perspective, every service page should include a unique title and meta description, a clear heading structure, relevant internal links to related services and locations, strong calls to action, and any supporting elements such as FAQs, reviews, or service-specific schema where appropriate. Each page should have a clear purpose, satisfy the user's search intent, and provide enough unique value to stand on its own.

### 4. What should every city page contain?

Every city page should demonstrate that the company actively serves the community while helping users find the services they need. It should include an overview of the services offered in that city, links to the primary service categories, information about the areas and neighborhoods served, locally relevant content, customer reviews or testimonials when available, frequently asked questions, and clear calls to action. If the city has a physical office, the page should also include the office address, hours, contact information, and an embedded map.

The goal of a city page is not to repeat the same content with a different city name, but to provide meaningful local context that builds trust and supports local search intent. Every city page should serve as the central hub for that market, connecting users to the appropriate services while reinforcing the company's presence in the community.

### 5. When is AI-generated content acceptable?

AI-generated content is acceptable when it is used to improve efficiency, not replace expertise. AI can help create first drafts, organize information, expand on ideas, and maintain consistency across websites, but every page should be reviewed, edited, and enhanced to ensure it is accurate, helpful, and reflects the company's brand, experience, and local knowledge. The final content should always provide value that goes beyond what AI can generate on its own.

Content should never be published simply because it was generated by AI. If the output is generic, repetitive, inaccurate, or fails to satisfy the user's search intent, it should be rewritten or combined into a stronger page. The standard should be that AI assists the content creation process, while humans remain responsible for the quality, originality, and usefulness of the final content.

### 6. What should AI never generate?

AI should never generate content that is inaccurate, misleading, fabricated, or presented as fact without verification. It should not invent customer reviews, certifications, awards, statistics, pricing, service offerings, employee information, local experience, or any other claims that cannot be supported by the business. AI should also avoid creating large amounts of repetitive or low-value content simply to target additional keywords or increase page count.

Every piece of AI-assisted content should be grounded in real business information and reviewed for accuracy before publication. The goal is to create helpful, trustworthy content that reflects the company's actual expertise and services, not to automate content for the sake of filling pages or manipulating search rankings.

## Section 6: Local SEO

### 1. How do you think about Google Business Profile and the website working together?

Google Business Profile and the website should work together as a single local SEO strategy rather than as separate assets. The Google Business Profile helps the business appear in local search and Google Maps, while the website provides the detailed information that builds trust, answers customer questions, and supports organic rankings. Both should consistently represent the same services, service areas, business information, and brand messaging.

The website should reinforce the topics and locations highlighted in the Google Business Profile, while the Google Business Profile should direct users to the most relevant pages on the website. When both are aligned, they strengthen each other's authority, improve the user experience, and increase the likelihood of earning both local map visibility and organic search traffic.

### 2. How do you choose service areas?

Service areas should be selected based on where the business can realistically and consistently provide service, not simply on where it wants to rank. Priority should be given to cities with a physical office, strong operational coverage, high demand, and meaningful revenue opportunity. Additional service areas should expand outward logically from each office to create a geographically connected service area rather than isolated or unrealistic markets.

The website, Google Business Profile, and overall local SEO strategy should all reflect the same service area strategy. Choosing service areas that align with the business's actual operations helps create a better user experience, supports stronger local relevance, and ensures the business can consistently deliver on the expectations set by its online presence.

### 3. What makes a location page valuable?

A valuable location page does more than mention a city, it demonstrates that the business genuinely serves that community and helps users find the information they need. It should provide meaningful local context, showcase the services available in that area, connect users to relevant service pages, answer common questions, and make it easy to contact or schedule service. If the location has a physical office, the page should also include the address, hours, contact information, and other location-specific details.

A location page should serve as the central hub for that market, not as a duplicate of other city pages with only the city name changed. Its purpose is to satisfy local search intent, build trust with potential customers, and strengthen the relationship between the business, its services, and the communities it serves.

### 4. What makes a location page unnecessary?

A location page is unnecessary when it does not provide any unique value beyond what already exists on the website. This commonly occurs when the page targets the same search intent as another page, contains mostly duplicate content with only the city name changed, or represents a location that is already covered by the homepage. Creating pages solely to increase the number of indexed pages rarely benefits users or long-term SEO performance.

Every location page should have a clear purpose and justify its existence by serving a distinct market or audience. If the page cannot provide meaningful local information, support a unique search intent, or improve the user experience, it is generally better to strengthen an existing page rather than create another low-value location page.

## Section 7: Technical SEO

### 1. Which technical SEO items do you always check before launch?

Before launch, every website should be reviewed to ensure search engines can properly crawl, index, and understand the site. This includes verifying page titles and meta descriptions, heading structure, canonical tags, robots.txt, XML sitemaps, structured data, internal linking, image optimization, redirects, URL structure, mobile usability, page speed, and Core Web Vitals. It's also important to confirm that all staging settings have been removed and that the site is fully crawlable and indexable.

In addition to technical validation, every page should be checked for broken links, duplicate content, missing metadata, and proper tracking implementation, including Google Analytics, Google Search Console verification, and any required conversion tracking. A thorough pre-launch review helps prevent technical issues that could negatively impact rankings, indexing, or user experience from day one.

### 2. Which technical issues concern you the most?

The technical issues that cause the greatest concern are those that prevent search engines from crawling, indexing, or properly understanding the website. This includes broken internal links, incorrect redirects, duplicate content, missing or incorrect canonical tags, blocked pages in robots.txt, noindex tags left on important pages, poor internal linking, and XML sitemap issues. These problems can significantly limit a site's ability to rank regardless of how strong the content is.

Performance and user experience are also critical. Slow page speed, poor Core Web Vitals, mobile usability issues, and large amounts of unnecessary JavaScript can negatively impact both rankings and conversions. The goal is to build websites that are technically sound from launch, allowing search engines to efficiently crawl the site while providing users with a fast, reliable, and seamless experience.

### 3. What should always be automated?

Anything that is repetitive, standardized, or required on every page should be automated whenever possible. This includes generating page titles and meta descriptions from templates where appropriate, canonical tags, XML sitemaps, robots.txt, structured data, breadcrumbs, internal navigation, image optimization, redirects, and consistent page layouts. Automating these elements improves consistency, reduces human error, and makes the platform easier to scale across multiple brands.

Automation should handle the technical framework, while content and strategic decisions remain intentional. The goal is to eliminate repetitive manual work so teams can focus on creating high-quality content, improving the user experience, and making informed SEO decisions rather than managing tasks that can be reliably handled by the platform.

### 4. What should always be manually reviewed?

Anything that affects content quality, user experience, or business accuracy should always be manually reviewed before launch. This includes page content, internal linking, calls to action, service descriptions, location information, business details, metadata, images, and any AI-generated content. These elements require human judgment to ensure they are accurate, helpful, align with the brand, and satisfy the user's search intent.

Technical automation should reduce repetitive work, but it should never replace quality assurance. Every website should undergo a final SEO review to verify that pages are technically sound, content is unique and valuable, links function correctly, and the overall site delivers a consistent experience for both users and search engines.

## Section 8: Framework Philosophy

### 1. What should every website built from this framework include?

Every website built from this framework should include a strong SEO foundation that can be consistently applied across multiple brands. This includes a logical URL structure, optimized page templates, responsive design, clean navigation, internal linking, XML sitemaps, robots.txt, canonical tags, structured data, image optimization, and fast page performance. The framework should also support scalable content generation for services, locations, blogs, FAQs, and other reusable content without sacrificing quality or user experience.

Beyond the technical foundation, every website should provide helpful, trustworthy content that reflects the brand it represents. The framework should make it easy to maintain consistent branding, SEO best practices, and conversion-focused design while remaining flexible enough to support different markets, service offerings, and future business growth. The goal is to create websites that are scalable, maintainable, and built to perform well in both search engines and for the users they serve.

### 2. What should never be customized?

The core architecture of the framework should never be customized on a per-website basis unless there is a clear business or SEO justification. This includes the URL structure, page hierarchy, navigation patterns, internal linking strategy, structured data implementation, technical SEO standards, and other foundational elements that ensure consistency across every website. Keeping these components standardized makes the framework easier to maintain, scale, and improve over time.

Customization should focus on the brand itself, including its messaging, visual identity, services, locations, and locally relevant content. By keeping the underlying framework consistent while allowing flexibility in the content and branding, each website can maintain its unique identity without sacrificing long-term SEO performance or maintainability.

### 3. What should always be configurable?

The framework should allow any brand-specific or location-specific information to be easily configured without requiring changes to the underlying code or site architecture. This includes branding, logos, colors, contact information, service offerings, office locations, service areas, business hours, reviews, calls to action, media, and other content that varies from one website to another. Content modules such as FAQs, promotions, financing options, and team information should also be configurable so each website accurately represents the business it serves.

The goal is to separate content from the framework. The framework should provide a consistent foundation, while configuration controls the information displayed on each website. This approach makes it easy to launch new websites, onboard acquisitions, update existing brands, and scale the platform without rebuilding core functionality.

### 4. What should future developers understand before making changes?

Future developers should understand that the framework is designed around long-term scalability, consistency, and SEO best practices. Changes should be evaluated based on their impact across the entire platform, not just a single website. A modification that solves a problem for one brand may introduce maintenance challenges, inconsistent user experiences, or SEO issues across every site built from the framework.

Before making changes, developers should understand the reasoning behind the existing architecture and follow established standards whenever possible. New features should improve the framework as a whole, remain backward compatible, and preserve the consistency that allows websites to be maintained, expanded, and optimized efficiently over time.
